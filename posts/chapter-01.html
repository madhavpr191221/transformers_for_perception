Chapter 1 — Why Vision?

Transformers and Perception: A Vision-First Approach

Author

Madhav Prashanth Ramachandran

1 A Satellite Passes Over

A satellite passes over the Deccan Plateau at 705 kilometres above sea level.

Its sensors record reflected electromagnetic radiation across dozens of spectral bands — visible, near-infrared, shortwave infrared. The result is a raster of numbers. A matrix, really, where each entry encodes how much energy bounced off a patch of ground roughly 30 metres wide. That matrix is an image.

Somewhere inside it is a dry riverbed. A village. A field of sorghum at different stages of growth. A road that does not appear on any map. None of that is written in the numbers. And yet — within seconds — you can look at that image and know exactly what you are seeing.

This book is about how that happens.

More precisely, it is about how to build machines that can do the same — not just for satellite imagery, but for any image of any scene, from any camera, in two dimensions or three.

The tool we will use, primarily, is the transformer. But we will not arrive at the transformer the way most books do — through language, through tokens, through the mechanics of text generation. We will arrive at it through vision. Through the problem of perception. Through the question of what it actually means for a machine to understand an image.

This is not merely a pedagogical choice. It is a claim: that vision is the right entry point for understanding transformers, and that starting here produces a deeper understanding than starting with language.

2 The Central Problem

Here is the central problem of visual perception, stated as plainly as I can manage.

You live in a three-dimensional world. Every camera, every retina, every imaging sensor collapses that world onto a two-dimensional surface. A scene with depth, occlusion, lighting, and geometry becomes a flat array of numbers. Information is destroyed in the process — irreversibly.

And yet, from that impoverished projection, you effortlessly recover depth, identity, motion, and meaning. You do this so automatically that it does not feel like computation. But it is computation. Extraordinarily sophisticated computation that your visual system performs without any apparent effort.

The projection problem, stated formally.

Let \(\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) be a point in the physical world. Let \(\mathbf{p} \in \mathbb{R}^2\) be its image on a sensor. The mapping \(\pi: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2\) that takes world points to image points is called projection. It is many-to-one — an infinite ray of world points maps to the same image point. The inverse problem — recovering \(\mathbf{P}\) from \(\mathbf{p}\) — is fundamentally ill-posed without additional constraints.

Vision is, at its core, the study of how to impose those constraints intelligently.

The figure below makes this concrete. A three-dimensional scene — objects at different depths, partially occluding each other — collapses onto a two-dimensional image plane. The depth information is gone. The occlusion relationships are partially recoverable. The geometry is distorted by perspective.

Notice what the slider demonstrates. As the image plane moves, the projected positions of A′, B′, and C′ shift — the same object in the world casts a different image depending on the geometry of the sensor. The world has not changed. Only the measurement has.

This is the first lesson of computer vision: an image is not the scene. It is a measurement of the scene, from a particular vantage point, under a particular projection.

Everything we do in this book — every architecture, every representation, every attention mechanism — is ultimately an attempt to invert this measurement.

3 Why Images?

Before we go further, it is worth asking a more basic question: why start with images at all?

The honest answer is personal. I have spent years working with sensor data — satellite imagery, multi-spectral rasters, LiDAR point clouds. For me, the physical world has always been the primary object of interest, and images have always been the primary data type. Language describes the world. Images, in some sense, are the world — or at least, they are its most direct measurement.

But there is also a mathematical argument, and it is worth making explicitly.

An image is a function. A grayscale image is a function \(I: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\) where \(\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) is the image domain — the set of pixel coordinates — and \(I(x, y)\) is the intensity at location \((x, y)\). An RGB image is a function \(I: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^3\). A multi-spectral satellite image is a function \(I: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^B\) where \(B\) is the number of spectral bands.

This function-theoretic view will be developed carefully in Chapter 2. For now, the key point is that images live in a space with rich mathematical structure — spatial relationships, local correlations, scale-dependent features, geometric transformations — and that structure will constrain everything we do with them.

Language lacks most of this structure. Text is a sequence of discrete tokens. The relationship between adjacent tokens is statistical, not geometric. There is no notion of spatial proximity, no natural notion of scale, no continuous domain over which functions vary smoothly. This is not a criticism of language — it is simply a description of its geometry.

When you work with images, you are always working in contact with the physical world. The numbers in an image are not arbitrary symbols. They are measurements of light, which is a physical quantity, governed by the geometry of projection, the physics of reflection, and the optics of the sensor. This contact with physical reality is, I find, one of the most satisfying aspects of working with visual data — and one that will become increasingly important as we move into Part VI (geometry) and Parts VII–VIII (3D vision and neural scene representations).

4 Why the Physical World Is Fundamentally 3D

We keep saying the world is three-dimensional. This seems obvious — and it is. But it is worth pausing to understand what this actually means for vision, because the consequences are non-trivial and will reappear throughout the book.

4.1 Depth is unobservable from a single image

A single camera image destroys depth. As we saw in Section 1.2, projection is a many-to-one map: every point along a ray through the camera centre projects to the same pixel. You cannot recover depth from a single image without additional assumptions — about the shape of the world, the reflectance properties of surfaces, the statistics of natural scenes.

This is not a limitation of any particular camera. It is a mathematical fact about projection. It is why depth estimation from monocular images is still an active research problem, and why systems that need reliable depth — autonomous vehicles, robotic manipulation, AR/VR — generally use stereo cameras, LiDAR, or structured light.

4.2 Shape, not colour, defines objects

Consider the two objects below.

Drag the light direction slider. The pixel values change dramatically — the raw numbers in the image are completely different — but the geometry is unchanged. Both objects are spheres. The shape is constant; the image varies with illumination.

This is why raw pixel values are, in general, poor representations. A recognition system that memorises pixel values will fail as soon as the lighting changes. A system that learns to represent shape — the underlying 3D geometry — will be robust. We will return to this idea repeatedly when we discuss representations (Chapter 3) and self-supervised learning (Chapter 17–19).

4.3 3D structure is the invariant

The key insight is this: the semantic content of a scene — what objects are there, where they are, what they are doing — lives in the 3D structure of the world, not in the 2D projection. The image is an observation. The 3D scene is the reality.

This is why the most powerful visual representations — the ones that generalise across viewpoints, lighting conditions, and sensors — tend to be those that explicitly or implicitly represent 3D structure. It is why Part VI of this book (Chapters 25–30) covers camera geometry, projective transformations, and multi-view reconstruction before we discuss 3D Vision Transformers. The geometry is not optional enrichment. It is load-bearing.

5 Why This Book Starts With Vision

There are two ways to teach transformers.

The standard way is to start with language. You introduce tokens, embeddings, the self-attention mechanism, and the encoder-decoder architecture. Then, having established the machinery, you show that the same ideas transfer to images — with some modification (patch embeddings instead of word embeddings, positional encodings adapted for 2D grids).

This book takes the other path. It starts with vision and stays there.

The reason is not that language is unimportant. Language models are remarkable, and some of the most important advances in deep learning have come from language modelling. The reason is that vision is, mathematically, a richer starting point.

Consider what you need to understand to follow a vision transformer from first principles:

  • What is an image? What does it mean to represent one mathematically?
  • What is a good representation of visual content? What properties should it have?
  • Why does weighted averaging of features make sense as an operation on visual data?
  • How does the attention mechanism relate to classical methods in image processing and computer vision?
  • What does it mean for a patch embedding to encode spatial information?

None of these questions have obvious analogues in language. But they all have deep connections to linear algebra, geometry, signal processing, and physics — connections that make the mathematics feel necessary rather than arbitrary.

A note on the treatment of language.

Language will appear in this book — as analogy, as contrast, occasionally as application. But it will never be the primary object of study. When a concept from NLP illuminates a vision concept, we will make the connection explicit. When it does not, we will not manufacture one.

There is also a personal reason, which I gave in the preface. When I worked through the Vision Transformers paper, something clicked that had not clicked before. The mathematics was identical to what I had seen in NLP — same attention mechanism, same residual structure, same positional encodings. But changing the modality changed everything about how I reasoned through the details.

Patches instead of tokens. Spatial structure instead of sequence. A concrete physical interpretation at every step.

I have tried to write the book I wish I had found.

6 The Arc of This Book

Let me close this chapter by sketching the intellectual arc of what follows.

Part I (Chapters 1–6) builds attention from scratch, without transformers. We start with images as mathematical objects, ask what a good representation is, and derive attention as a natural consequence of the need to compute context-dependent weighted averages over a set of feature vectors. The transformer appears at the end of Chapter 5 — not as an architecture introduced from outside, but as the logical conclusion of a line of reasoning we have been developing since Chapter 1.

Part II (Chapters 7–12) builds Vision Transformers. Having established the mathematical foundations, we ask how to apply them to images. Patch embeddings, positional encodings, residual connections, layer normalisation — each is derived from first principles, not imported as a component. Part II ends with the Swin Transformer, which introduces local attention and hierarchical representations.

Parts III and IV (Chapters 13–19) apply the machinery to detection, segmentation, and self-supervised learning. These chapters are more applied in character, but the mathematical foundations laid in Parts I and II will be visible throughout.

Part V (Chapters 20–24) asks a different kind of question: attention is useful, but can we afford it? The quadratic complexity of self-attention is a real constraint, and this part covers the main approaches to approximating or avoiding it — Linformer, Performer, FlashAttention, KV-caching.

Part VI (Chapters 25–30) is where the book makes its most distinctive move. Having spent five parts building up to a complete understanding of 2D vision transformers, we leave the image plane entirely and enter the geometry of the physical world. Cameras, perspective projection, homogeneous coordinates, stereo vision, multi-view geometry, Structure from Motion, Lie groups. This part is harder than the rest, and more rewarding.

Parts VII and VIII (Chapters 31–40) put geometry and transformers together. Point clouds, PointNet, Point Transformers, multi-view transformers, Neural Radiance Fields, Gaussian Splatting, dynamic worlds, world models. By the end, the transformer is no longer a black box that processes image patches. It is a tool for reasoning about 3D structure, motion, and scene understanding.

The figure is not decorative. Notice that the bars get taller as the book progresses. Parts I–V are cumulative but not dramatically harder than what came before. Part VI is a step change — the geometry is genuinely more demanding, and the reader who arrives there without the foundations of Parts I–V will struggle. This is intentional. The geometry is placed after the transformers, not before, because the whole point is to understand what it means to apply transformer-style reasoning to 3D data. You need both halves.

7 What You Should Already Know

This book is not an introduction to machine learning. I am going to assume the following:

Linear algebra. Vector spaces, matrix multiplication, eigendecompositions, SVD. If the phrase “the column space of \(A\) is orthogonal to the null space of \(A^T\)” makes sense to you, you are fine.

Calculus and basic analysis. Partial derivatives, the chain rule, a passing familiarity with the idea of a function space. Measure theory is not required.

Probability and statistics. Random variables, expectations, conditional distributions, basic properties of the Gaussian. If you have read through a graduate ML course, you are well-equipped.

Some exposure to deep learning. You should know what a neural network is, what backpropagation does, and have a rough sense of what CNNs do to images. You do not need to be an expert.

What I will not assume is prior knowledge of transformers, computer vision, or geometry. Those are the subjects of this book.

A note on notation.

I will try to be consistent throughout. Vectors are bold lowercase: \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^D\). Matrices are bold uppercase: \(\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}\). Sets are calligraphic: \(\mathcal{X}\). Functions are italic: \(f: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}\). Scalars are plain italic: \(n, D, \lambda\). I will annotate matrix dimensions at first use and whenever there is room for confusion.

8 Summary

This chapter has done three things.

First, it grounded the entire enterprise in a concrete physical reality: images are projections of a three-dimensional world onto two-dimensional sensors, and understanding vision means learning to reason about that projection.

Second, it argued for starting with vision rather than language — not as a stylistic choice, but as a mathematical one. Images have geometric structure, physical grounding, and a natural connection to 3D geometry that makes the mathematics of attention feel necessary rather than arbitrary.

Third, it sketched the arc of the book: from mathematical foundations in Part I, through Vision Transformers in Part II, through efficiency and applications in Parts III–V, to the geometry and 3D vision of Parts VI–VIII.

In Chapter 2, we make the first mathematical move: we ask what an image actually is, as a mathematical object, and what that implies for how we should process it.

Let us begin.
